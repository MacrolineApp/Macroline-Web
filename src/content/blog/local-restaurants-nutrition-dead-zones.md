Search any tracker for a McDonald's item and you'll find official numbers — chains over a certain size are legally required to publish them. Now search for the farmers omelette at the diner two blocks from your house.

Nothing. Or worse than nothing: five contradictory user entries, none sourced, one of them clearly describing a different omelette.

This is the dead zone. Independent restaurants serve a huge share of American meals and publish approximately zero nutrition data — no law requires them to, and most never will. Every tracker handles this the same way: a pile of anonymous user submissions with no provenance, quietly mixed in with the real data until you can't tell which is which.

## What we do instead

When a Macroline user logs a dish from a local spot — say, described to the AI as "4-egg farmers omelette with ham and cheddar from a diner" — a few careful things happen:

1. **It's yours first.** The dish saves as *your* private food, marked `unverified`. It shows up in your searches, your history, your re-logs. It does not silently become everyone's truth.
2. **It joins a queue, not the catalog.** Branded local dishes also enter a review pool. If three different people independently log the same omelette at the same diner with similar numbers, that agreement means something — and the cluster shows us exactly how much they agree.
3. **A human promotes it — with the label it deserves.** When an entry graduates to the shared catalog, it's published as what it is: an estimate, tier-labeled, never dressed up as official. If the restaurant ever posts real numbers, we attach the source and *then* the tier upgrades. Corroboration raises confidence; it doesn't manufacture a source.

We promoted the first batch of these recently — thirty-some dishes from local restaurants that exist in no other database. Greek salads from a coney island. A seafood pasta from a neighborhood pizzeria. Street tacos from a taqueria. Small data, but data nobody else has, labeled honestly.

## Why the labels are the point

Every tracker has *some* local-restaurant data. What none of them will tell you is which entries are somebody's guess. Our bet is that the label is worth more than the illusion: you make better decisions knowing "this is a solid estimate" than believing a fiction with four decimal places.

So order the omelette. Describe it honestly. You might be the person who puts your diner on the map.
